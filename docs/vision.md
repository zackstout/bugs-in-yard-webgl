# Vision

## What This Is

A personal natural history collection, made navigable through space.

The subject is insects observed in one place — a yard, a patch of land — photographed over time. The photographs are the primary artifact. Everything else exists to help you find them, understand what you are looking at, and feel the shape of the collection as a whole.

---

## The Problem With Existing Tools

Photo apps organize by date or album. They treat a photograph of a beetle the same as a photograph of a birthday cake. There is no concept of biological relationship, no way to see that the two wasps you photographed are more closely related to each other than either is to the bee two photos over.

iNaturalist knows the taxonomy. It does not make the photographs feel like a collection. It makes them feel like a database.

A specimen cabinet does neither of these things. You open a drawer and the relationships are immediately visible — beetles on the left, moths on the right, the variation within a genus arranged in a row. The organization is the navigation. You do not search. You browse.

This application is a specimen cabinet. The photographs are the specimens. The space is the drawer.

---

## The Experience

You open the application and see the whole collection at once — a field of photographs arranged in space, loosely clustered by what they are. Beetles here, bees there, moths further out. The clusters are not labeled yet. You get a sense of proportion: there are many more beetles than anything else. One cluster is very small, maybe two or three photographs. You are curious about it.

You move toward that small cluster. The photographs resolve. Labels appear. You can see they are all flies — something in the family Syrphidae, hoverflies. You did not know you had hoverflies. There are three photographs, all from the same week in July. You select one. It expands. The metadata appears beside it: the date, the place, the scientific name. The photograph is sharp. You can see the wing venation.

You press Escape. The camera pulls back. You navigate toward the beetle cluster, which is much larger. As you enter it, it breaks apart into subclusters — families. You move toward one. Genera appear. You find a genus you have photographed many times, six or seven images. They are spread across two summers. You can see the variation in markings between individuals.

At no point did you use a search box. At no point did you page through a grid. You moved through a space that was organized by biology, and the biology revealed itself as you approached.

---

## What Makes It Different

**Space encodes meaning.** Two photographs that are close together are biologically related. Distance is not arbitrary. The layout is not aesthetic decoration — it is the primary information structure.

**Zoom controls what you know.** Far away, you know the order. Closer, the family. Closer still, the genus and species. The information density matches your proximity. You do not get flooded with detail you are not ready for.

**Photography is primary.** The photographs are not thumbnails that lead somewhere else. They are the thing. Metadata and taxonomy serve the photograph, not the other way around.

**The collection has a shape.** You can see at a glance that you photograph more beetles than anything else, that your hoverfly collection is small, that there is a gap where Lepidoptera should be. The shape of what you have observed is visible. So is the shape of what you have not.

---

## What It Is Not

It is not a social platform. There are no other users, no comments, no likes. It is one person's collection.

It is not a field guide. It does not try to be comprehensive. It contains exactly what was observed and photographed, nothing more.

It is not a data visualization. The goal is not to produce charts or statistics about the collection. The photographs are not data points. They are records of specific encounters with specific animals.

It is not a mobile app. The experience requires a mouse and a screen large enough to feel like a space. A phone grid view is available as a fallback, but it is not the experience.

---

## The Standard

The right question to ask at any decision point is: **does this serve the photographs?**

A feature that makes the collection easier to navigate but draws attention to itself rather than to the insects is wrong. An effect that is impressive but pulls the eye away from the photography is wrong. A label system that floods the view with text before you need it is wrong.

The photographs are of real animals, observed in a real place. The application should feel like it was built in their service.
